# Animal Farm: An Overview, a Review, and My Take

> *“The creatures outside looked from pig to man, and from man to pig, and from pig to man again; but already it was impossible to say which was which.”*
>
> — George Orwell, *Animal Farm* (1945)

Of everything in the book, that line is my favourite. It is the last sentence George Orwell wrote in *Animal Farm*, and in a way it sums up the whole lesson of the book. But to see why it hits so hard, we need the background. What actually happened?

---

## The Background: A Farm, a Drunkard, and a Dream

There was a farm, the **Manor Farm**, near the village of Willingdon in England. It belonged to **Mr. Jones**, a farmer who had once been capable but had become a careless drunkard. He neglected his land, his men and his animals. The animals worked from dawn to dusk, got barely enough food to stay alive, and when they stopped being useful they were sold or slaughtered.

Then there was **Old Major**, a prize Middle White boar (he had been exhibited at shows under the name *Willingdon Beauty*). He was twelve years old, wise, and deeply respected by every animal on the farm. One night Old Major had a strange dream. After Mr. Jones had staggered drunk to bed, all the animals gathered in the big barn to hear him. They came in one by one: the three dogs Bluebell, Jessie and Pincher; the pigs; the hens and pigeons; the sheep and cows; the two huge cart-horses, **Boxer** and **Clover**; **Muriel** the white goat; **Benjamin** the old, cynical donkey; **Mollie**, the vain white mare who pulled Mr. Jones's trap; and the cat, who found the warmest place and purred through the whole thing.

Somehow, somewhere, one night, a revolution was conceived. This is what Old Major said:

> "Comrades, you have heard already about the strange dream that I had last night. But I will come to the dream later. I have something else to say first. I do not think, comrades, that I shall be with you for many months longer, and before I die, I feel it my duty to pass on to you such wisdom as I have acquired. I have had a long life, I have had much time for thought as I lay alone in my stall, and I think I may say that I understand the nature of life on this earth as well as any animal now living. It is about this that I wish to speak to you.
>
> "Now, comrades, what is the nature of this life of ours? Let us face it: our lives are miserable, laborious, and short. We are born, we are given just so much food as will keep the breath in our bodies, and those of us who are capable of it are forced to work to the last atom of our strength; and the very instant that our usefulness has come to an end we are slaughtered with hideous cruelty. No animal in England knows the meaning of happiness or leisure after he is a year old. No animal in England is free. The life of an animal is misery and slavery: that is the plain truth.
>
> "But is this simply part of the order of nature? Is it because this land of ours is so poor that it cannot afford a decent life to those who dwell upon it? No, comrades, a thousand times no! The soil of England is fertile, its climate is good, it is capable of affording food in abundance to an enormously greater number of animals than now inhabit it. This single farm of ours would support a dozen horses, twenty cows, hundreds of sheep—and all of them living in a comfort and a dignity that are now almost beyond our imagining. Why then do we continue in this miserable condition? Because nearly the whole of the produce of our labour is stolen from us by human beings. There, comrades, is the answer to all our problems. It is summed up in a single word—Man. Man is the only real enemy we have. Remove Man from the scene, and the root cause of hunger and overwork is abolished for ever.
>
> "Man is the only creature that consumes without producing. He does not give milk, he does not lay eggs, he is too weak to pull the plough, he cannot run fast enough to catch rabbits. Yet he is lord of all the animals. He sets them to work, he gives back to them the bare minimum that will prevent them from starving, and the rest he keeps for himself. Our labour tills the soil, our dung fertilises it, and yet there is not one of us that owns more than his bare skin. You cows that I see before me, how many thousands of gallons of milk have you given during this last year? And what has happened to that milk which should have been breeding up sturdy calves? Every drop of it has gone down the throats of our enemies. And you hens, how many eggs have you laid in this last year, and how many of those eggs ever hatched into chickens? The rest have all gone to market to bring in money for Jones and his men. And you, Clover, where are those four foals you bore, who should have been the support and pleasure of your old age? Each was sold at a year old—you will never see one of them again. In return for your four confinements and all your labour in the fields, what have you ever had except your bare rations and a stall?
>
> "And even the miserable lives we lead are not allowed to reach their natural span. For myself I do not grumble, for I am one of the lucky ones. I am twelve years old and have had over four hundred children. Such is the natural life of a pig. But no animal escapes the cruel knife in the end. You young porkers who are sitting in front of me, every one of you will scream your lives out at the block within a year. To that horror we all must come—cows, pigs, hens, sheep, everyone. Even the horses and the dogs have no better fate. You, Boxer, the very day that those great muscles of yours lose their power, Jones will sell you to the knacker, who will cut your throat and boil you down for the foxhounds. As for the dogs, when they grow old and toothless, Jones ties a brick round their necks and drowns them in the nearest pond.
>
> "Is it not crystal clear, then, comrades, that all the evils of this life of ours spring from the tyranny of human beings? Only get rid of Man, and the produce of our labour would be our own. Almost overnight we could become rich and free. What then must we do? Why, work night and day, body and soul, for the overthrow of the human race! That is my message to you, comrades: Rebellion! I do not know when that Rebellion will come, it might be in a week or in a hundred years, but I know, as surely as I see this straw beneath my feet, that sooner or later justice will be done. Fix your eyes on that, comrades, throughout the short remainder of your lives! And above all, pass on this message of mine to those who come after you, so that future generations shall carry on the struggle until it is victorious.
>
> "And remember, comrades, your resolution must never falter. No argument must lead you astray. Never listen when they tell you that Man and the animals have a common interest, that the prosperity of the one is the prosperity of the others. It is all lies. Man serves the interests of no creature except himself. And among us animals let there be perfect unity, perfect comradeship in the struggle. All men are enemies. All animals are comrades."
>
> At this moment there was a tremendous uproar. While Major was speaking four large rats had crept out of their holes and were sitting on their hindquarters, listening to him. The dogs had suddenly caught sight of them, and it was only by a swift dash for their holes that the rats saved their lives. Major raised his trotter for silence.
>
> "Comrades," he said, "here is a point that must be settled. The wild creatures, such as rats and rabbits—are they our friends or our enemies? Let us put it to the vote. I propose this question to the meeting: Are rats comrades?"
>
> The vote was taken at once, and it was agreed by an overwhelming majority that rats were comrades. There were only four dissentients, the three dogs and the cat, who was afterwards discovered to have voted on both sides. Major continued:
>
> "I have little more to say. I merely repeat, remember always your duty of enmity towards Man and all his ways. Whatever goes upon two legs is an enemy. Whatever goes upon four legs, or has wings, is a friend. And remember also that in fighting against Man, we must not come to resemble him. Even when you have conquered him, do not adopt his vices. No animal must ever live in a house, or sleep in a bed, or wear clothes, or drink alcohol, or smoke tobacco, or touch money, or engage in trade. All the habits of Man are evil. And, above all, no animal must ever tyrannise over his own kind. Weak or strong, clever or simple, we are all brothers. No animal must ever kill any other animal. All animals are equal."

Notice the last paragraph in particular. *"In fighting against Man, we must not come to resemble him."* Hold on to that sentence. The whole book is the story of how it was broken.

---

## From a Speech to a Rebellion

Old Major then told them about the dream: a vision of the earth as it would be when Man had vanished. It reminded him of an old song his mother used to sing, **"Beasts of England"**. He taught it to them, and the animals sang it again and again until the whole farm was roaring it out, and Mr. Jones, thinking a fox had got into the yard, fired his gun into the darkness.

**Three nights later, Old Major died peacefully in his sleep.** It was early March.

His speech set off a chain of events. For the next three months there was a great deal of secret activity. The work of teaching and organising fell naturally to the pigs, who were generally recognised as the cleverest of the animals. Three young pigs stood out:

- **Snowball**, lively, inventive and a gifted speaker;
- **Napoleon**, a large, rather fierce-looking Berkshire boar, not much of a talker but with "a reputation for getting his own way";
- **Squealer**, a small, fat pig and a brilliant talker, who (the animals said) *"could turn black into white."*

These three worked Old Major's teachings into a complete system of thought, which they called **Animalism**. Not everyone was convinced. Some animals talked of loyalty to Mr. Jones. Mollie only wanted to know whether there would still be sugar and whether she could still wear ribbons in her mane (Snowball told her the ribbons were "the badge of slavery"). **Moses**, Mr. Jones's tame raven, a spy and a teller of tales, kept talking about **Sugarcandy Mountain**, a paradise in the clouds where animals went when they died. The pigs had to argue hard against him. Their most faithful disciples were Boxer and Clover, who could not think things out for themselves but absorbed everything the pigs taught and passed it on to the others in simple words.

**The Rebellion** came far sooner than anyone expected, and almost by accident. On **Midsummer's Eve** Mr. Jones went into Willingdon, got drunk at the Red Lion and did not come home until midday on Sunday. His men milked the cows and went off rabbiting without feeding the animals. Jones came back and fell asleep on the sofa. By evening the animals were starving. One of the cows broke in the door of the store-shed with her horn, and the animals began helping themselves from the bins. When Jones and his four men came in lashing with whips, the hungry animals turned on them, butting and kicking, and the men fled. Mrs. Jones threw a few things into a carpet bag and slipped away. Moses flapped off after her, croaking loudly.

**Manor Farm belonged to the animals.**

Their first acts were full of joy and symbolism. They threw the bits, nose-rings, dog-chains and the cruel knives Jones used to castrate pigs and lambs down the well. The reins, halters, blinkers and nosebags went on the rubbish fire, and the whips with them. Snowball also burned the ribbons, because ribbons "should be considered as clothes, which are the mark of a human being." They toured the farmhouse in awe and agreed that it should be kept as a museum and that **no animal must ever live there**. Snowball painted out *MANOR FARM* on the five-barred gate and painted **ANIMAL FARM** in its place.

---

## The Laws: The Seven Commandments

The pigs then revealed that over the past three months they had taught themselves to read and write from an old spelling book that had belonged to Mr. Jones's children. They had reduced the principles of Animalism to **Seven Commandments**, which Snowball painted in large white letters on the tarred wall of the big barn. They would form "an unalterable law by which all the animals on Animal Farm must live for ever after":

1. **Whatever goes upon two legs is an enemy.**
2. **Whatever goes upon four legs, or has wings, is a friend.**
3. **No animal shall wear clothes.**
4. **No animal shall sleep in a bed.**
5. **No animal shall drink alcohol.**
6. **No animal shall kill any other animal.**
7. **All animals are equal.**

Old Major's full list of forbidden human habits had been longer. He had also said that no animal must ever **live in a house**, **smoke tobacco**, **touch money** or **engage in trade**. Those did not make it onto the wall, but at the beginning everyone understood them to be part of Animalism.

Most of the animals could not read well enough to learn all seven by heart. Some could not read at all: Boxer never got past the letter D, and Mollie learned only the letters of her own name. So Snowball boiled the whole thing down to a single maxim:

> **"Four legs good, two legs bad."**

(The birds objected that they had only two legs. Snowball explained that a bird's wing is "an organ of propulsion and not of manipulation," so it counts as a leg.) The sheep loved the maxim and would bleat it for hours at a time, and that turned out to be very useful to somebody later on.

## However, It Also Led To…

However, the Rebellion also led somewhere else: **a new ruling class.**

The warning sign came on the very first evening. The cows had not been milked and were in pain, so the pigs milked them, producing five buckets of frothing milk. "What is going to happen to all that milk?" someone asked. Napoleon placed himself in front of the buckets and said, *"Never mind the milk, comrades! The harvest is more important."* When the animals came back in the evening, **the milk had disappeared.** Soon it came out that the milk, and the windfall apples too, were going into the pigs' mash. Squealer explained that pigs were "brainworkers", that science had proved milk and apples essential to their health, and that they were only eating them *for the sake of the other animals*. After all, if the pigs failed in their duty,

> *"Jones would come back! Yes, Jones would come back! Surely, comrades, … there is no one among you who wants to see Jones come back?"*

Nobody wanted Jones back, so nobody objected. That line, **"Surely you don't want Jones back?"**, became the pigs' all-purpose weapon.

From there the story moves step by step.

**The good times (briefly).** The first harvest was a triumph. Boxer took as his personal motto **"I will work harder!"** A flag was made, a green tablecloth with a white hoof and horn painted on it. Sunday meetings were held where the animals debated and voted on resolutions (only the pigs ever proposed any). Snowball formed endless committees: the Egg Production Committee for the hens, the Clean Tails League for the cows, the Whiter Wool Movement for the sheep, and the Wild Comrades' Re-education Committee, which tried to tame rats and rabbits and failed. Meanwhile, quietly, **Napoleon took the nine newborn puppies of Jessie and Bluebell away "to educate them"** and kept them hidden in a loft.

**The Battle of the Cowshed.** In October, Jones came back with his men and some men from the neighbouring farms, **Foxwood** (owned by the easy-going **Mr. Pilkington**) and **Pinchfield** (owned by the hard, shrewd **Mr. Frederick**). Snowball had studied a book about Julius Caesar's campaigns. He planned the defence, led the charge and was wounded by Jones's gun. Boxer fought like a stallion. The men were routed. Snowball and Boxer were both decorated **"Animal Hero, First Class"**, and a sheep killed in the battle received **"Animal Hero, Second Class"** posthumously. Jones's gun was set up at the foot of the flagstaff, to be fired twice a year: on 12 October, the anniversary of the battle, and on Midsummer Day, the anniversary of the Rebellion.

**Mollie runs away.** She was caught letting one of Mr. Pilkington's men stroke her nose, and soon afterwards she vanished. She was later seen between the shafts of a smart dog-cart outside a pub in Willingdon, with a scarlet ribbon in her forelock, looking perfectly contented.

**The windmill and the coup.** Snowball and Napoleon disagreed about everything. The biggest split was over Snowball's plan to build a **windmill** that would generate electricity, light and heat the stalls, and cut the working week to three days. Napoleon was against it. (He went to look at Snowball's chalk drawings of the plans and urinated on them.) There were two slogans: *"Vote for Snowball and the three-day week"* and *"Vote for Napoleon and the full manger."* On the day of the vote, Snowball's speech had swung the meeting his way. Then Napoleon stood up and let out a high-pitched whimper, and **nine enormous dogs in brass-studded collars**, the puppies now fully grown, burst into the barn and chased Snowball off the farm. He escaped through a hole in the hedge and was never seen again.

Napoleon then **abolished the Sunday debates**. From now on all questions would be decided by a special committee of pigs, with himself presiding, and the animals would simply receive their orders. Four young porkers tried to protest. The dogs growled, and the sheep drowned the protest with "Four legs good, two legs bad!" Squealer explained that "no one believes more firmly than Comrade Napoleon that all animals are equal," but that the animals might make the wrong decisions. *"Discipline, comrades, iron discipline!"* Boxer added a second motto: **"Napoleon is always right."**

Old Major's skull was dug up and set on a stump beside the gun, and the animals were made to file past it reverently each Sunday. Three weeks later, Napoleon announced that **the windmill would be built after all**. Squealer explained that it had really been Napoleon's idea from the start and that Snowball had stolen it. Napoleon had only *pretended* to oppose it, as a manoeuvre to get rid of Snowball. *"Tactics, comrades, tactics!"*

**Work, trade and the farmhouse.** The animals worked like slaves: a sixty-hour week, plus "voluntary" Sunday work, where any animal who stayed away had its rations cut by half. Boxer got up earlier and earlier to haul stone for the windmill. Then Napoleon announced that the farm would **trade with neighbouring farms** through a solicitor called **Mr. Whymper**, and money came onto Animal Farm. The animals had an uneasy memory of a resolution never to engage in trade or use money, but Squealer assured them that no such resolution had ever been passed or even written down. It was a lie "probably traceable to Snowball." Next, **the pigs moved into the farmhouse** and slept in the beds.

**The storm and the scapegoat.** In November a violent gale knocked the half-built windmill to the ground. Napoleon stood over the ruins and announced who was responsible: **Snowball**, who had sneaked in by night and destroyed it out of spite. He sentenced Snowball to death in absence. From then on Snowball got the blame for everything: broken windows, blocked drains, lost keys, the milk going sour. Soon Squealer announced "documents" proving Snowball had been Jones's secret agent all along, even at the Battle of the Cowshed. Boxer, for once, was troubled: *"I do not believe that. Snowball fought bravely at the Battle of the Cowshed."* When he was told that Comrade Napoleon had stated it "categorically," he gave in. *"If Comrade Napoleon says it, it must be right."*

**The hens' rebellion.** In the hungry winter, Napoleon signed a contract to sell **four hundred eggs a week**. The hens, led by three young Black Minorca pullets, protested by flying up to the rafters and laying their eggs there, where they smashed on the floor. Napoleon stopped their rations and ordered that any animal who gave them so much as a grain of corn would be punished by death. They held out for five days, and **nine hens died**.

**The purge.** Then came the most horrifying scene in the book. Napoleon called the animals together, wearing both his medals (he had recently awarded himself "Animal Hero, First Class" and "Animal Hero, Second Class"). The dogs seized the **four pigs** who had protested against abolishing the meetings. The pigs confessed that they had been in secret contact with Snowball, and the dogs tore their throats out. Three dogs also attacked Boxer, who pinned one of them to the ground under his hoof and let it go only when Napoleon told him to. Then more animals came forward to confess:

- the **three hens** who had led the egg rebellion said Snowball had appeared to them in a dream and incited them to disobey;
- a **goose** confessed to hiding six ears of corn and eating them in the night;
- a **sheep** confessed to urinating in the drinking pool, at Snowball's urging;
- **two other sheep** confessed to murdering an old ram, a devoted follower of Napoleon, by chasing him round and round a bonfire while he had a cough.

**All of them were slaughtered on the spot.** Afterwards there was a pile of corpses at Napoleon's feet and "the air was heavy with the smell of blood, which had been unknown there since the expulsion of Jones."

The shaken animals huddled on the knoll. Clover looked down at the farm, and Orwell gives us her thoughts:

> *"These scenes of terror and slaughter were not what they had looked forward to on that night when old Major first stirred them to rebellion."*

She could not put it into words, but she knew she had pictured a society of animals free from hunger and the whip, all equal, each working according to their capacity, the strong protecting the weak. Instead, "no one dared speak his mind," fierce dogs roamed everywhere, and comrades were torn to pieces after confessing to shocking crimes. So they began to sing "Beasts of England". Squealer immediately came to announce that **"Beasts of England" was now banned**. It had been the song of the Rebellion, and *"the Rebellion is now completed."* In its place came a new song by the pig-poet **Minimus**, beginning *"Animal Farm, Animal Farm, / Never through me shalt thou come to harm!"*

**The cult of the Leader.** Napoleon became "our Leader, Comrade Napoleon." He was given titles like *Father of All Animals, Terror of Mankind, Protector of the Sheep-fold, Ducklings' Friend*. He ate alone off Jones's Crown Derby china, was served by the dogs, and had a gun fired on his birthday. Minimus wrote a poem, *Comrade Napoleon*, which was painted on the barn wall under a portrait of the Leader.

**The Battle of the Windmill.** Napoleon played Pilkington and Frederick off against each other and finally sold a pile of timber to Frederick, who paid in banknotes that turned out to be **forgeries**. Soon after, Frederick attacked with his men and **blew up the rebuilt windmill with blasting powder**. The animals drove the men off, but at terrible cost: a cow, three sheep and two geese were killed and nearly everyone was wounded. Boxer's hoof was split. Squealer called it a victory, the gun was fired, and Napoleon created a new decoration, the **Order of the Green Banner**, and awarded it to himself.

**Boxer's fate.** This part of the book always hurts the most. Boxer, now nearly twelve and past retirement age, wanted only to see the windmill well under way before he retired. (The animals had been promised pensions: retirement at twelve for horses and pigs, fourteen for cows, nine for dogs, seven for sheep and five for hens and geese.) One day he collapsed while hauling stone. Squealer announced that Napoleon was sending him to the best hospital in Willingdon. A van came to take him away. It was **Benjamin**, the cynical donkey who never showed emotion, who galloped up braying at the top of his voice and read the lettering on the side:

> ***"Alfred Simmonds, Horse Slaughterer and Glue Boiler, Willingdon. Dealer in Hides and Bone-Meal. Kennels Supplied."***

*"They are taking Boxer to the knacker's!"* The animals screamed at Boxer to get out. They heard his hooves drumming weakly inside the van, but the strength had gone out of him, and he was never seen again. Three days later Squealer announced that Boxer had died peacefully in hospital, his last words being *"Long live Comrade Napoleon! Napoleon is always right."* The van, he said, had once belonged to the knacker, and the vet had simply not painted the old name out yet. That same night a grocer's van delivered a large wooden crate to the farmhouse. There was loud singing and quarrelling from inside, and word went round that **the pigs had got the money to buy themselves another case of whisky**.

Old Major had warned Boxer, *"the very day that those great muscles of yours lose their power, Jones will sell you to the knacker."* It was not Jones who sold him.

**Years pass.** Muriel, Bluebell, Jessie and Pincher died. Jones died in an inebriates' home. Snowball was forgotten. No animal ever actually retired. The windmill was finally finished, but it was used for milling corn for profit, not for electricity or a three-day week. The farm grew richer, and none of the animals except the pigs and dogs grew any richer with it. Squealer explained that the pigs worked hard on mysterious things called "files," "reports," "minutes" and "memoranda." Most of the animals could no longer remember what life had been like before the Rebellion, so they had nothing to compare their lives with.

## The Pigs Became Corrupt: How the Laws Were Changed

Is *corrupt* the right word? I think it is. Power corrupted the pigs. But the milk and apples on the very first evening suggest that some of them only needed the chance. What makes it so frightening is not that the pigs broke the Commandments. It is **how** they did it. They never repealed a law in public. Each time, a law was quietly *edited* at night. When the animals thought they remembered something different, one of the few who could read would go and look at the wall. There it was in writing, so they concluded their memory must be wrong.

Here is every change:

| # | Original Commandment | What it became | When and why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | *No animal shall sleep in a bed.* | **No animal shall sleep in a bed *with sheets*.** | When the pigs moved into the farmhouse and began sleeping in the beds. Squealer: a bed is merely a place to sleep, and a pile of straw is a bed; the rule was against *sheets*, "which are a human invention." The pigs had removed the sheets and slept between blankets. |
| 6 | *No animal shall kill any other animal.* | **No animal shall kill any other animal *without cause*.** | After the purge, when the traitors had been executed. Now there had been "good reason" for killing them. |
| 5 | *No animal shall drink alcohol.* | **No animal shall drink alcohol *to excess*.** | After the pigs found Jones's whisky in the cellar. Napoleon was seen galloping round the yard in Jones's old bowler hat, and next morning (hung over) he was believed to be dying. A decree made drinking alcohol punishable by death, but within days he was ordering booklets on brewing and distilling, and the paddock meant as a grazing ground for retired animals was ploughed up to grow barley. One night the animals found Squealer sprawled at the foot of a broken ladder beside the barn wall, with a lantern, a paintbrush and an overturned pot of white paint. Only Benjamin understood, and he said nothing. |
| 1, 2, 3, 7 | *Whatever goes upon two legs is an enemy. / Whatever goes upon four legs, or has wings, is a friend. / No animal shall wear clothes. / All animals are equal.* | **Every Commandment was erased.** In their place, one sentence: **ALL ANIMALS ARE EQUAL BUT SOME ANIMALS ARE MORE EQUAL THAN OTHERS.** | The final chapter. Squealer had taken the sheep away for a week to "teach them a new song." Then one evening Clover let out a terrified neigh: **a pig was walking on his hind legs.** It was Squealer, and then came Napoleon himself, upright, **with a whip in his trotter**, the dogs frisking around him. Clover asked Benjamin to read the wall to her. It was the one time he broke his rule. |
| — | *Four legs good, two legs bad.* | **Four legs good, two legs *better*!** | The new song the sheep had learned. They bleated it for five minutes the moment the pigs appeared on two legs, which killed any protest before it could start. |

The unwritten laws from Old Major's speech went the same way, one after another:

- **No animal must ever live in a house.** The pigs moved into the farmhouse.
- **No animal must ever touch money or engage in trade.** Mr. Whymper came every Monday, and Squealer said the resolution against trade had never existed.
- **No animal must ever wear clothes.** After the pigs walked upright they took Jones's clothes out of the wardrobes. Napoleon appeared in a black coat, ratcatcher breeches and leather leggings, and his favourite sow in the watered-silk dress Mrs. Jones used to wear on Sundays.
- **No animal must ever smoke tobacco.** Napoleon took to smoking a pipe in the garden.
- **No animal must ever tyrannise over his own kind.** (See everything above.)
- **Beasts of England**, the song of the revolution, was banned.
- The pigs gave themselves a radio, a telephone, and subscriptions to *John Bull*, *Tit-Bits* and the *Daily Mirror*.

At the last dinner, Napoleon hosted the neighbouring farmers. He told them the farm would be run on sound business lines, and announced that:

- the animals would **no longer call each other "Comrade"**;
- the custom of **marching past Old Major's skull** would stop, and the skull had already been buried;
- the **hoof and horn would be removed from the flag**, leaving it plain green;
- and the farm would no longer be called Animal Farm. It would go back to its "correct and original name": **The Manor Farm.**

Mr. Pilkington raised a toast and joked, *"If you have your lower animals to contend with, we have our lower classes!"* The men were impressed, above all, that the animals on this farm worked harder and ate less than on any other farm in the county.

---

## Pig to Man, and Man to Pig

Then the card game began. A little later a violent quarrel broke out, because Napoleon and Mr. Pilkington had each played an **ace of spades** at the same time. Both were cheating. The animals crept up to the farmhouse window and looked in at the pigs and the men shouting at each other:

> *"Twelve voices were shouting in anger, and they were all alike. No question, now, what had happened to the faces of the pigs. The creatures outside looked from pig to man, and from man to pig, and from pig to man again; but already it was impossible to say which was which."*

This is what leads to the quote at the top, my favourite. The circle is complete. The farm has the same name, the same masters and the same misery. Only the faces at the top have changed, and by the end even that difference is gone. Old Major warned them: *"In fighting against Man, we must not come to resemble him."* The pigs did not simply fail to keep that warning. They **became** the thing they had fought.

## My Take

**1. The real story behind the fairy story.** Orwell subtitled the book *A Fairy Story*, but it is a very pointed allegory of the Russian Revolution and the Soviet Union under Stalin. He wrote it between 1943 and 1944, while Britain and the USSR were wartime allies. Several publishers turned it down for that reason (T. S. Eliot at Faber and Faber among them), and it finally came out in August 1945 from Secker & Warburg. The mapping is hard to miss:

| In the book | In history |
|---|---|
| Mr. Jones | Tsar Nicholas II and the old regime |
| Old Major | Marx (and Lenin) |
| Animalism | Communism |
| The Rebellion | The Russian Revolution of 1917 |
| Napoleon | Joseph Stalin |
| Snowball | Leon Trotsky, driven into exile and blamed for everything |
| Squealer | The propaganda machine (*Pravda*) |
| The dogs | The secret police (NKVD) |
| Boxer | The loyal, exploited working class |
| The sheep | The unthinking masses who repeat slogans |
| Moses and Sugarcandy Mountain | The Church, first opposed and then tolerated because it was useful |
| Mollie | The comfort-loving middle and upper classes who fled |
| Benjamin | The sceptic who sees everything and does nothing |
| Battle of the Cowshed | The Civil War and foreign intervention (1918–21) |
| The windmill | The Five-Year Plans |
| The hens' rebellion | Peasant resistance to forced collectivisation |
| The confessions and executions | The Moscow show trials and the Great Purge (1936–38) |
| Mr. Frederick / Pinchfield | Hitler and Nazi Germany (the timber deal is the 1939 Nazi–Soviet Pact; the Battle of the Windmill is the 1941 invasion) |
| Mr. Pilkington / Foxwood | Britain and the Western powers |
| The final card game | The Tehran Conference (1943), with the allies already on the edge of the Cold War |

**2. But the book is bigger than Russia.** That is why it still works today. It is about how *any* revolution, movement or institution can decay: how language is bent ("readjustment" instead of "reduction"), how memory is rewritten, how an outside enemy (Jones) and an inside traitor (Snowball) are used to silence every question, and how a slogan chanted loudly enough ends a debate. Orwell himself said in a 1946 letter that the moral was that revolutions only bring real improvement when the masses are alert and know how to throw out their leaders once those leaders have done their job. He called the pigs keeping the milk and apples for themselves the turning point of the story. I agree. Everything after that is just the slope getting steeper.

**3. The failure is shared.** It is too easy to blame only the pigs. The animals *let* it happen. Boxer's "I will work harder" and "Napoleon is always right" are touching, and they are also a disaster: loyalty with no questions attached. Clover sensed something was wrong but could not find the words. Benjamin could read the wall perfectly well and chose to stay silent until it was too late. The sheep did not have to understand anything to be useful; they only had to be loud. Tyranny in *Animal Farm* is not only something done *to* the animals. It is built, one small silence at a time, *with* them.

**4. Why the ending is perfect.** The final line is not a dramatic punishment or a second revolution. It is a quiet recognition. The animals are outside, looking through a window at their masters, exactly where they were in chapter one. That is what makes it devastating, and it is why it is my favourite line: it takes the whole book to earn it, and it needs only one sentence to say it.

> *"He who fights with monsters should be careful lest he thereby become a monster. And if thou gaze long into an abyss, the abyss will also gaze into thee."*

**Selah!**
